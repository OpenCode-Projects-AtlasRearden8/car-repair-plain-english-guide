# Decode the Estimate

An estimate is a menu of charges written for people who already know the vocabulary. This page is the vocabulary. Bring it with you when the service desk slides the paper across the counter.

## The anatomy of a typical estimate

```
        INVOICE / ESTIMATE  —  CUSTOMER COPY
Line  Item               Qty   Rate      Amount
 1    Diagnostics fee     1    $150      $150.00
 2    Brake pads (fr)     2    $58.00    $116.00
 3    Brake labor (fr)   2.5 hr $120/hr  $300.00
 4    Brake rotors (fr)   2    $95.00    $190.00
 5    Rotor resurface     2    $30.00    $60.00
 6    Shop supplies       —     8%       $65.00
 7    Haz waste fee       —    flat      $12.00
 8    Core charge         —    refund    $100.00*
 9    Fluid disposal      —    flat      $8.00
      Subtotal                        ~$1,001.00
      Sales tax (parts)                 $32.00
      ESTIMATE TOTAL                  ~$1,033.00
```
*A core charge is a deposit you get **back** when you return the old part.*

Every line above gets explained below. If a line on your real estimate is not explained by something on this page, that is exactly the kind of item you politely ask about (see [Confident Questions](questions.md)).

## The line items, decoded

### 1. Diagnostics fee
The charge for finding out what's wrong. Mechanics get paid to investigate — running code readers, road-testing, chasing electrical gremlins — and that time isn't free. $75–$200 for a few hours of diagnostic time is normal and fair; $150+ just to *read a stored code* is a question worth asking.

- **Fair:** an hour or more of hands-on diagnosis, especially for intermittent problems.
- **Worth asking about:** a "diagnostic fee" that turns out to be a 5-minute code read. Reading codes is often free at parts stores.
- **Key question:** *"Does the diagnostic fee go toward the repair if I approve it?"* Many shops credit it; many don't. That answer changes the total.

### 2. Parts lines
Each part usually shows **quantity, unit price, and sometimes a brand**. Three things to know:

- **OEM** ("Original Equipment Manufacturer" / dealer parts) come from the carmaker. Priciest, guaranteed to fit, often unnecessary.
- **Aftermarket** parts are made by other companies. Quality ranges from *excellent* (many brands actually supply the carmakers) to *terrible*. Mid-grade aftermarket for brakes, filters, and sensors is usually the smart-money choice.
- **Used / recycled / salvage** parts are secondhand. Great for expensive, low-wear items (radios, wheels, body panels), riskier for things that wear out (clutches, turbos).

**Key question:** *"What brand parts are these, and what's the warranty on parts and labor?"* Cheapest is not automatically wrong — but you should know you're getting it.

### 3. Labor — and "flat rate"
Labor is usually billed in **shop hours** times an **hourly rate** ($100–$200/hr at independent shops, more at dealers). Two systems exist:

- **Actual time:** you pay for real hours worked. Simple, but rare.
- **Flat rate (the norm):** the shop uses a guidebook that says, for example, "front brake pads = 2.5 hours" for your model, whether it takes the tech 90 minutes or 4 hours. If the tech is fast, the shop makes money; if slow, you **still pay the flat 2.5** — but never more than the guidebook said.

**What to check:** the book hours are standard and searchable, so you can sanity-check them. If a line says 6 hours of labor for a job other sources call 2, ask. Also watch for **overlap** — "replace X, and Y while we're in there" should cost *less combined labor* than doing them separately, because half the work is shared.

### 4. "While we're in there" items
Often genuine money-savers: if the bumper has to come off anyway, replacing a worn 4-hour-accessory belt for $30 of parts and 15 minutes of shared labor is a good deal. But bundle creep is real. Ask: *"Which of these become unsafe or much more expensive if I do them later instead?"* That separates "smart to co-op now" from "nice to have."

### 5. Shop supplies / environmental fees
Small percentage (often 2–9%) added on top to cover rags, solvents, waste-oil disposal. Usually legitimate and rarely negotiable in any meaningful way. **But check the math:** supplies charged as a percent should generally apply to *parts only*, not parts + labor, or the percentage inflates fast.

### 6. Hazardous-waste / fluid-disposal fees
Flat fees ($5–$15) for legally disposing of old oil, coolant, and batteries. Normal. If you see *both* a percent-based "shop supplies" *and* several separate disposal fees *and* a hazmat line, ask what each one covers — they shouldn't all be billing the same oil barrel.

### 7. Core charge
A refundable deposit on recyclable parts (batteries, alternators, brake calipers). You pay it now and get it back when you hand over the old part. It is **not** an extra cost if you remember to ask for your old parts back.

**Tip:** always say *"I'd like my old parts back, please."* A shop that resists that request for a worn part is one you should be more careful with.

### 8. Sales tax
Many states tax parts but not labor; some tax everything. Just verify the tax rate matches your locality. That's it — this line is rarely a problem.

## Red flags vs. green flags

| Green flag (normal, fair) | Red flag (ask hard questions) |
| --- | --- |
| Diagnostic fee, credited toward repair | Diagnostic fee charged "just to hook up a code reader" |
| Brand-name or mid-grade aftermarket parts named | No brand names anywhere on the estimate |
| Flat-rate hours that match published guides | Labor hours that don't match any guide you can find |
| 8% shop supplies on parts only | Supplies/fees stacked on top of each other and on labor |
| Core charge with refund explained | Line items you cannot get a straight answer about |
| "Unsafe if not fixed" talk (brakes, tires, steering) | Vague "we found other stuff wrong" with no part numbers or symptoms |

## Reading between the lines: the big totals

- **Don't estimate-shop hourly rates in isolation.** A $95/hr shop doing the job in flat 2.0 hours beats a $70/hr shop billing 3.5.
- **A second opinion is almost always worth it for any job over ~$500.** It costs you an hour. Ask the second shop to *diagnose from scratch*, not to price the first shop's list — you want them to find what's actually wrong, not confirm assumptions.
- **Written estimates beat verbal ones.** Ask for the itemization in writing or email. It protects you *and* them.

## Escape-hatch phrase

If you're overwhelmed at the counter, you are allowed to say exactly this:

> "Thank you — I'm going to take this home and think about it tonight. Please don't start any work until I call back."

A good shop says "of course." Any pressure to decide instantly is your cue to keep driving (metaphorically) to another shop.
