# AI Quote Translator

You have a repair quote in front of you and a phone in your pocket. You can use free AI — on whatever app or website you already have — to turn the quote into plain English in about five minutes, then make a real decision instead of a panicked one. This page shows exactly how.

## What AI can and can't do here

**AI can:**

- Translate jargon (`"rear main seal replacement"` → "fixing the oil leak where engine meets transmission — labor-heavy job, usually $500–$1,500 because the transmission must be partially removed").
- Explain what a part does and why its replacement costs what it does.
- Flag estimate lines that look unusual (double-billed labor, bundle of unrelated parts, "sandblasting fuel rail" nonsense).
- Draft polite questions and a second-opinion script.
- Help you *think* about repair-now vs. wait.

**AI cannot:**

- Diagnose your car. It has never seen your car, and any concrete claim ("your catalytic converter is definitely bad") would be a guess dressed up as certainty.
- Guarantee prices — it quotes typical ranges; your region, vehicle, and year change real numbers.
- Replace a shop's hands-on test, a safety inspection, or a recall lookup.

**The whole trick:** treat AI as a translator and coach, never as the mechanic.

## The 5-minute method

Open your AI tool of choice. Paste the quote. Send a prompt from below. Read the answer. Ask the follow-ups. Done. The method has three passes; most people only need Pass 1.

### Pass 1 — Translate (do this always)

> Hi. I need help reading a car repair **estimate** before I decide whether to approve it. Please act as a plain-English translator — explain each line item simply, flag anything that looks odd, and do **not** diagnose my car. Ask me my make/model/ mileage and region before you estimate typical price ranges.
>
> My car: [year, make, model, mileage]
>
> Estimate:
> [PASTE the estimate exactly as written, line by line]
>
> Then return:
> 1. A table: line item — what it means in plain English — whether it's a typical charge.
> 2. Any lines that seem duplicative, unusually priced, or worth questioning, with the *exact question I should ask the shop*.
> 3. Divide the items into: **safety (fix now)** / **function (soon)** / **cosmetic or deferred (optional)** / **worth a second look**.
> 4. The total I'd pay if I did only safety items.

### Pass 2 — Interrogate (do this when the total feels heavy)

> Thanks. Now I want to pressure-test the estimate itself. For each of your "worth questioning" lines: as the shop, what would you answer to convince me it's genuinely needed? Then as a skeptical customer, tell me what I should still verify or ask in person. End with a shortlist of **3 questions** I should ask the service desk on the phone tomorrow morning.

### Pass 3 — Decide (do this to choose repair-now vs. wait)

> I want a decision framework, not a decision from you. My situation: [your region, whether the car is your only car, rough budget this month]. For the "function (soon)" items I haven't approved yet: for each one, tell me (a) what typically happens and typical rough cost if I wait 3 months, (b) what could go seriously wrong, (c) a rough "repair-now vs. wait" score. Never tell me "fix it" as a diagnosis — tell me the tradeoffs and then remind me a professional who's seen the car makes the final call.

## The repair-now vs. wait framework

When AI helps you sort the line items, check them against this simple table. *(This is the decision tree most of us are actually doing in our heads, made explicit.)*

| Category | Repair now? | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Safety** (brakes, tires, steering, fuel leaks, anything that affects stopping/steering) | **Yes, now** | This is rent-vs-own math that isn't negotiable. Doesn't need AI; just approve it. |
| **Money-expanding faults** (misfires, oil/coolant leaks, running lean/rich, overheating) | **Soon — days to a couple weeks** | Small jobs here become big jobs fast (a $30 plug becomes an $800 cat; a weeping hose becomes a warped head). The clock ticks while you drive. |
| **Comfort & cosmetic** (A/C, radio, trim, DOES-affect-emissions-only codes) | **Wait, plan, budget** | Nothing gets worse today. You can price-shop leisurely. |
| **Fail-eventually parts** (aging batteries, belts approaching mileage interval, sensors without symptoms) | **Wait until *just before* the milestone** | Do it at the recommended interval, not before; you may get full life out of the part. |
| **"Found more stuff" without a symptom or a test** | **Get a second opinion first** | Extra work discovered mid-job needs a phone call and written justification before approval, not a signature at pickup. |

Then the everyday budget tiebreakers, the way a financially level-headed friend would weigh it:

- **The car's remaining value.** A $1,800 repair on a car worth $3,500 is a real decision. The same repair on a car worth $9,000 is usually just "do it."
- **Whether you have (or can get) a reliable backup car** if this one's off the road for two days.
- **How much slack you actually have** this month — and whether an uncomfortable-but-real "not this week" answer from you is fine, because it *is* fine.

## Prompt for judging the shop's urgency claims themselves

The AI approach that feels uncomfortable but is worth one message:

> A shop says my [make/model, mileage] urgently needs [item] and quotes $[N]. Without diagnosing my specific car: what do reputable sources say about [item] — typical failure mileage, typical cost range, what the failure feels like if it does happen, and how urgent it actually is for safety? Then tell me exactly what I should ask the shop about this on the phone.

Notice what you're doing: getting the *general knowledge* that makes your follow-up question hard to bluff. ("How did you test that the [item] is failing?" lands very differently after you know how the test works.)

## What to bring back to the counter

After your AI session, you walk in with **three sentences**, not a lecture:

> "Thanks for the estimate. Three questions: first, what test confirmed the [part] is failing? Second, are the shop supplies calculated on parts only or parts and labor? Third, does the diagnostic fee credit toward the work if I approve today?"

You don't need to mention AI. It's your thinking tool, not a gotcha. (Also, using AI to prep for a negotiation is just as fair as reading The Consumer Reports used-car price guide first — this is homework, not trickery.)

## The honest AI disclaimer, restated

AI gave you generic knowledge about *a* kind of car. It didn't diagnose *your* car. The shop that touched it, or a second shop that inspects it, has information you and I never will. Use AI to ask sharper questions and to protect your peace of mind — not to override a professional safety call.
