# Confident Questions

You don't need to be a mechanic. You need to sound like a customer who will not be rushed. This is a page of copy-paste questions, organized by moment. Quiet confidence beats arguing.

## The mindset (read once, skip forever after)

- You are **not** being rude by asking questions. Shops that respect you welcome them; shops that don't have told you something important.
- "That's a little over what I was hoping to spend — let me think about it" is a **complete sentence**. You owe no explanation.
- The person at the counter is usually a service advisor, not the mechanic touching your car. They translate; sometimes badly. Ask them to write things down.

## 1. Before any work is approved

**On understanding the estimate:**

- "Can we go line by line through this? I'd like to understand what each item is before I decide."
- "What brand and quality level are the parts — OEM, aftermarket, or used? What's the parts and labor warranty?"
- "How many labor hours is this job, and does that match the standard flat-rate guide for my car?"
- "Are the shop supplies and fees in this total, or added on top? What do they apply to?"

**On the diagnosis itself:**

- "What test or measurement confirmed the failing part? Not just the code — what did you physically check?"
- "If the check-engine code just names a system, what ruled out the other causes?"
- "Can I see the old part or a photo of the damaged one when you have it?"

**On urgency:**

- "Which of these items are safety issues, which are 'it'll fail eventually,' and which are 'wait and watch'?"
- "What happens if I drive this car for two weeks without the repair?"
- "Is there any cheaper intermediate option to get me safely through the month while I plan for the full repair?"

**On money:**

- "Does the diagnostic fee apply toward the repair if I approve the work today?"
- "Is there any current special, or preferred-customer discount I should know about?"
- "What's the total out-the-door number — parts, labor, tax, everything?"

**On the exit ramp:**

- "I'm going to take this home and think about it. Please don't start any work until I call back."
- "Can you email me this written estimate?" *(A written itemized quote is normal and you should expect it.)*

## 2. When the price feels too high

- "Get me a quote doing this with mid-grade aftermarket parts instead of OEM and let me compare."
- "Can any of these jobs be safely deferred to the next visit so I can spread the cost out?"
- "Is there a shop or parts supplier you'd suggest if this were your money — I'm price-checking this week."
- "Could resurfacing instead of replacing apply anywhere here?" *(Brake rotors, for example — sometimes resurfacing existing parts is cheaper than new ones, sometimes not; the shop knows which cases apply.)*
- "What's included in that labor that I'd be paying twice if I did the related job separately next visit?"

## 3. When you want a second opinion

Say this at shop #2, and mean it:

> "I have a written estimate from another shop. I'd like you to **diagnose my car from scratch** — not price their list — and tell me what *you* find and what you'd charge. I'll pay for your diagnostic time if you don't do work today."

Key points in that script:

- You said *from scratch*, so they test instead of rubber-stamping.
- You offered to pay for their time, which changes the tone from "price-war shopping" to "professional consultation," and professionals respect that.
- You didn't name your budget. Let them make the first number.

Also good: "Is there anything on that list you *disagree* with, or would have found differently?" A tech with integrity will tell you which items are prepaid-opinion and which are measurement.

## 4. When work is done (before you pay)

- "Show me the old parts, please." *(Repeat your inner voice: you always take your old parts.)*
- "Walk me through the invoice line by line — did anything change from the estimate?"
- "What changed?" *(If the total went up: "I approved X, Y, Z at $N. Walk me through exactly what grew and why.")*
- "Does this fix come with a written warranty — parts, labor, and how long?"

Note: minor overruns due to hidden corrosion or extra rusted bolts happen and can be legitimate; big surges need to have been called in **before** the work was done, not discovered at the register.

## 5. The safety-not-negotiable list

Approve these immediately when a professional reports them; defer almost everything else:

- Brakes, brake lines, rotors that are near/below minimum thickness
- Tires worn to wear-bars, sidewall damage, tread separating
- Steering or suspension parts flagged as unsafe (ball joints, tie rods, control arm mounts)
- Coolant leaks that cause overheating, anything that risks fire
- Any recall work (recalls are **free at the dealer** — look up your VIN at the NHTSA recall lookup, government site, free)

## The "I left to think about it" script

Copy-paste the whole thing if helpful:

> Hi, this is [name], about my [year, make, model] — thanks for the estimate today. I want to think it over tonight. Don't start any work. I'll call back tomorrow.

That's it. This message is polite, complete, and buys you exactly one business day without ANY argument. Most big repair decisions benefit from exactly one night's sleep.

## Bonus: the phrase library for hard moments

| Situation | The phrase |
| --- | --- |
| Pressure to decide now | "Good work takes a good decision. I need tonight to think." |
| Vague "other stuff is wrong" | "Write down the part names and prices and I'll review tonight." |
| Upsell at the register | "That wasn't on the estimate. Please invoice only what I approved." |
| Jargon wall | "Say that again like I've never opened a hood. I'd genuinely like to learn." |
| Defensive shop | "I'm not questioning your skill — I just want the numbers in writing so I can compare." |
| You want to leave cordially | "Thanks. I'll shop this one around this week." |
